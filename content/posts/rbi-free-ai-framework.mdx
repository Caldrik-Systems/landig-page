For a year, the RBI FREE-AI framework has read mostly as intent. The FREE-AI Committee's report, released on August 13, 2025, gave Indian banks seven "sutras" and six pillars — public trust, disclosure, bias-testing, board-level accountability — but explicitly left the mechanics to future master directions and circulars. That deferred period is ending.

In late June 2026, the RBI released draft guidance on model risk management that reads like FREE-AI's principles translated into engineering requirements: board-approved **Model Risk Management Frameworks (MRMF)**, mandatory **human-override and kill-switch arrangements** for AI models, and seven named risk-control dimensions — explainability, hallucination boundaries, bias, overfitting, spurious correlations, output variability, and data drift. Comments closed July 24, 2026; the rules are not yet final, but the direction is no longer ambiguous.

The timing matters because of what banks are simultaneously trying to do. A FICCI-IBA-BCG report published August 12, 2026, *Winning in the AI Era*, found that GenAI adoption among Indian banks jumped from 10% of institutions running use cases in 2024, to 44% in 2025, and 86% this year — and it explicitly urges banks to move past copilots into agentic AI that redesigns unstructured, judgment-heavy workflows rather than just automating structured ones. Loan origination alone still runs through 25-plus documents and six to eight layers of manual review, with operating and collection costs eating 40–50% of what it costs to serve small-ticket credit. That's the productivity case agentic AI in banking is being sold on — extending past loan processing into fraud detection and collections. It's arriving at the exact moment regulators are specifying how autonomous decision systems must be governed.

## LLMOps for banks: why kill switches are an architecture decision, not a policy add-on

The instinct in a compliance-led organisation is to treat the MRMF draft as a documentation exercise — write the policy, file the disclosures, move on. That undersells what "kill-switch arrangements" actually demand of a system that takes autonomous action.

An agent that can initiate a transaction, adjust a credit limit, or trigger a collections workflow needs **interruptibility, state checkpoints, and audit logging built into its execution graph from the start**. Retrofitting a human-override point onto an agentic pipeline designed purely for throughput is closer to a rebuild than a patch, because it changes how the system holds state between tool calls — not just what dashboard sits on top of it.

This is the practical difference between AI engineering and AI strategy work: LLMOps for Indian banks increasingly means versioned model registries, human-in-the-loop checkpoints, and decommissioning hooks designed in, not audited in after deployment.

## The vendor accountability clause GCCs can't outsource away

One detail in the draft deserves more attention than it's getting: **independent validation is required even where a vendor has already certified a third-party model**. Combined with FREE-AI's emphasis on entity accountability, this closes a loophole many Indian banks lean on — treating a purchased or GCC-built model as someone else's compliance problem.

It isn't. Whatever the model's origin, the regulated entity carries the validation burden. That has direct implications for how banks structure vendor contracts and in-house GCC mandates going into 2027.

---

None of this is finalised, and the RBI could soften specific thresholds before issuing binding master directions. But the trajectory of the FREE-AI framework — from sutras to named technical controls in an active draft — is consistent enough that waiting for the final circular before building the underlying architecture looks like the riskier bet.
